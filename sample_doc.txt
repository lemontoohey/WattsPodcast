Hollow Silica Nanosphere Light Scattering

When light encounters a hollow silica nanosphere with a shell thickness of
approximately 20 nanometres and an outer diameter of 250 nanometres, the
scattering cross-section is dominated by the refractive index contrast
between the silica shell (n ~= 1.45) and the air-filled core (n = 1.0).

This contrast creates a Mie scattering regime where the particle behaves
as a resonant cavity for specific wavelengths of visible light, particularly
in the blue-to-green range (450-550 nm). The hollow core acts as a low-index
defect, concentrating the electromagnetic field at the shell-core interface
and enhancing backscatter efficiency by a factor of three to five compared
to solid silica spheres of equivalent total diameter.

In practical terms, this means that a coating formulated with these hollow
spheres can achieve the same opacity and brightness as a conventional
titanium dioxide coating while using roughly forty percent less raw material
by mass, because the scattering efficiency per particle is so much higher.

The trade-off is mechanical: hollow shells are more fragile under shear
during mixing, and the synthesis process (typically a sacrificial-template
method using polystyrene or calcium carbonate cores that are later dissolved
or calcined away) is more expensive per kilogram of finished pigment than
direct precipitation methods.

Current research is focused on shell-thickness uniformity, since variation
of even a few nanometres across a batch broadens the resonance peak and
reduces the overall whiteness index of the coating.
